### 1. THE CALLING RITUAL — a Philadelphia primary source that explains the 1904 "At Home" clipping

**[A] Louis Dreka, *Card Etiquette*, Philadelphia, 1873.** Published by the Chestnut Street engraver who actually supplied Philadelphia society with its cards. The title page reads "PUBLISHED BY Louis Dreka, Card Engraver and Stationer. 1033 Chestnut Street, PHILADELPHIA." and it is copyrighted "in the year 1873, by LOUIS DREKA."
URL: https://archive.org/details/drekascardetique00drek
- "Reception days are mentioned on Visiting Cards of those having stated reception days" — the specimen card printed beneath it uses a Philadelphia address, "Thursdays. 1364 Walnut Street."
- On why the card mattered at the front door: "You call on your friend, and if you have no Card you must trust your name to a servant, who, it is more than probable, will make a mistake, and your friend comes down under the impression that you are a stranger or a peddler."
- The four turned-down corners, engraved on the reverse: "on the upper left hand corner of the card is the word *Visite*; on the upper right corner, *Felicitation*; on the lower left corner, *Congé*; on the lower right corner, *Condolence*." Dreka adds that by 1873 "Turning down corners is not now much used, and disfigures the card."
- "Cards sent to friends before leaving for a journey, are issued with simply P. P. C. in lower left corner" — this is the mechanical link between the calling ritual and the seasonal migration below.

**[A] Mrs. H. O. Ward, *Sensible Etiquette of the Best Society*, Philadelphia: Porter & Coates, tenth revised edition, 1878.** A Philadelphia-published manual, chapter "CALLS AND CARDS."
URL: https://archive.org/details/sensibleetique00bloo
- On the servant's role on an At Home day (p. 76): "On reception days, the well-drilled servant in American cities receives the card of the caller on a small salver, and, of course, suppresses it, because the card is only desired by the hostess to refresh her memory as to who has been present at her reception."
- On the collision of At Home days (p. 76–77): "Where a lady has one day in every week, and this day for some reason is an inconvenient one to some of her friends, whose day may be the same, for instance, cards ought not to be sent through others, but the call made upon some other day."
- On the servant screening callers at the door (p. 101): "a well-trained servant soon learns to discriminate between those ladies who are calling merely to leave their cards and those who are really desirous of being admitted."
- Note the American/English contrast the book draws (p. 76): the London footman "is trained to announce the name distinctly as he ushers the caller into the room where his mistress is receiving," whereas in America "the servant has not had previous instructions… in reference to his mistress being at home, or not at home, or engaged."

**Why this matters for 1822 Pine:** the archive's Dec 11, 1904 Inquirer line — "At home—Mrs. Henry C. Davis and the Misses Davis, of 1822 Pine street" — is not a social nicety, it is a published notice of a stated reception day, exactly the institution Dreka's specimen card and Ward's chapter describe. **[B]** The connection between the clipping and the ritual is inference, clearly labelled; the ritual itself is A-grade and Philadelphia-sourced.

### 2. THE PLAN — double parlours, service stair, dumbwaiter, speaking tube, bells

**[A] Samuel Sloan, *City and Suburban Architecture*, Philadelphia: J. B. Lippincott, 1859.** Sloan was the leading Philadelphia architect of exactly this moment. Design II is titled "A CITY RESIDENCE" and is described as "adapted in width to suit the almost universal demand for the erection of buildings in immediate contact with each other."
URL: https://archive.org/details/gri_33125013609264 (Design II, pp. 23–27)

- Double parlour, verbatim (p. 25): "The drawing-room, which is divided by columns and an elliptical archway, is lighted by two windows in front and one on the rear; each division is about sixteen by twenty-three feet." Sloan then specifies "An oval centre-piece of three and a half by six feet will be placed in each division of the drawing-room" and "Marble mantles of approved pattern will be provided and set one in each division of the drawing-room."
- Service stair, verbatim (p. 25): "Rear of this vestibule is a flight of stairs for the use of servants, under which are the stairs leading to the cellar."
- Servants' hall and dumbwaiter, verbatim (p. 25): "Beyond the arrangements already described are the servants' hall or living room, the kitchen and laundry. Accessible from the kitchen, through the servants' hall, is the butler's pantry, in which is a sink, and a dumb-waiter, which communicates with the pantry above, adjoining the principal dining-room."
- Note the dining room is on the **second** floor in this plan, not the first — the dumbwaiter exists because the kitchen is below and behind, and the dining room is above and in front.
- Bells and speaking tube (p. 41, "IRON-WORK" of another design): "The front door-bell will require a pull to correspond with the knob of the door, and four inside bells are necessary; also, a speaking-tube from the pantry to the kitchen; all to be properly arranged in working order."
- Hierarchy of finish, made explicit in hardware (same spec): "The knobs of the front and vestibule doors, and all the hinges… will be silver-plated; the other knobs for the first story, and all in the second, will be the best quality of white porcelain; the knobs for the basement and attic will be mineral."

**Caveat [B]:** Sloan's Design II is 25 ft front × 50 ft deep; 1822 Pine is 22 ft × 90 ft to Waverly. Sloan documents the *type* and the *period expectation*, not this house. Do not publish Sloan's room dimensions as this house's.

### 3. HEAT, GAS, WATER — the 1859 Philadelphia baseline

**[A] Sloan, same volume, p. 74 (essay on warming and ventilation):**
- "The days of capacious fire-places and blazing hearths seem to have about departed. More convenient and economical modes have been successfully sought. Heating by furnaces in the cellar has become quite common, and in view of its many advantages it seems probable that a few more years will make it almost universal in city residences."
- "A flue warming each room by a register near the floor, ventilates it by one placed near the ceiling."
- Plumbing spec (p. 27): "Water will be introduced through suitable pipes to the kitchen-sink and bath-rooms… The bath-tubs and sinks lined with copper, tinned and planished… A galvanized iron circulating boiler and water-back will be attached to the kitchen-range, to supply hot water for the sinks, bath-tubs, and wash-basins."
- Gas spec (p. 27), room by room: "Gas will be introduced, through pipes of sufficient size, to all the rooms. A drop-light will be placed in the front vestibule and two in the hall, as also at each landing of the stairs. Each division of the drawing-room will have a chandelier with twelve burners, and two side-lights… and the kitchen, laundry, and servants' hall each a drop and side-light. All other rooms, including baths and butler's pantry, will have one burner each."
- Philadelphia hook (p. 87): the furnace Sloan recommends "is the invention of Mr. Thomas T. Tasker, of Philadelphia" — i.e. Morris, Tasker & Co., a local ironworks.

**[A] A dated Philadelphia gas price, from a newspaper page that also names this house** — see finding 5 below. The Cresson Gas Regulator advertisement on that page prints an office's actual meter readings and the rate: gas "at 3 30 per thousand (including United States tax, 80 per M.)" in 1866.

**[B] Philadelphia Gas Works — Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.** https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/philadelphia-gas-works/ — founded 1836; employees "lit 46 gas street lamps on Second Street" on February 8, 1836; "in March 1841 the Philadelphia government took over ownership of PGW"; "Private use of gas lighting grew more slowly because the cost of lighting homes was beyond the reach of the city's working class"; "By 1890, 1,500 public lights lit Philadelphia's streets and two million lights lit private homes."

### 4. SERVANTS — Du Bois and Isabel Eaton counted them on THIS street

This is the strongest lens finding available, and it is free and republishable.

**[A] W. E. B. Du Bois, *The Philadelphia Negro: A Social Study*, Philadelphia, 1899.**
URL: https://archive.org/details/philadelphianegr00dubo
- The study area, verbatim (p. 1): "The work commenced with a house-to-house canvass of the Seventh Ward. This long narrow ward, extending from South Seventh street to the Schuylkill River and from Spruce street to South street, is an historic centre of Negro population." **[B] inference, clearly labelled:** Pine Street lies between Spruce and South, and 1822 Pine is west of 7th, so this house sits inside Du Bois's canvass district. That is a geographic inference from the stated boundaries, not a statement that this house was canvassed.

**[A] Isabel Eaton, "Special Report on Negro Domestic Service in the Seventh Ward, Philadelphia," bound as pp. 425–509 of the same volume.**
- **Table IX, "Distribution of Colored Service with Reference to the Fashionable Quarter" (p. 451)** counts servants street by street. On Pine Street: "99 domestics, or 58 per cent of all colored domestic servants on Pine Street" in the seven blocks east of Broad, against "71 domestics, or 42 per cent of all colored domestic servants on Pine Street" in the seven blocks west. Eaton's reading (p. 451): "The smaller number of colored domestics employed in the fashionable section is noticeable both on Pine and Spruce streets." **[B]** the 1800 block falls inside the seven blocks west of Broad; Eaton does not enumerate the blocks, so say "within the western count," not "on this block."
- Eaton's explanation, quoting a Philadelphia butler (p. 450): "You see they (the employers) go to Europe and bring home Englishmen, and that knocks out 'the Negro.'" And: "it seems probable that the fashion of importing English and French service has an appreciable effect."
- **The staffing grades of a Philadelphia house (p. 444):** "If another grade be supplied between the second and third given here — a grade found in well-to-do Philadelphia families, where two women servants are employed — this grading of London service applies very fairly to the condition of colored service in Philadelphia." Eaton then describes "the large number of houses where two women servants are kept, cook and 'second girl'… and finally, the establishments with many domestics, each having his or her own special duties."
- **What a single servant actually did**, in her own words (p. 444): the one woman does the "cooking, washing, ironing, and drags up all the ashes, tends furnace, cleans the front, and does every single thing." (Note "tends furnace" and "drags up all the ashes" — this is the human cost of Sloan's cellar furnace.)
- **Living in (Table XI, p. 454):** of colored domestics in the ward, "only 50 per cent of the colored women in service and only 24 per cent of the colored men lodge at their employers' establishments." Among single women, "only 38.7 per cent of them going home at night."
- **Sub-occupations (Table V, p. 445):** Butler 109, Coachman 76, Waiter 387, Cook 47, Valet 4 (male); Chambermaid 114, Waitress 44, Child's nurse 21 (female).
- **Thursday was the day out.** Du Bois, p. 205: "Thursday afternoons and evenings, when the servant girls are free, are always sure to have some sort of entertainment." And in a footnote (p. 231): "The members of the Berean Union have opened a 'Y' parlor, where young colored girls employed as domestics can spend their Thursday afternoon both pleasantly and profitably. The parlor is open from 4 until 10 p. m., every Thursday."
- On the shape of the working day (p. 458): "Household work is a ceaseless round, which like woman's work, is 'never done.' And the private domestic, even when given considerable liberty and free time while within the household, must always hold himself in readiness to answer any call at a moment's notice."

### 9. THE SEASONAL HOUSE

**[A] The mechanism, in the Philadelphia etiquette literature.** Dreka (1873): "Cards sent to friends before leaving for a journey, are issued with simply P. P. C. in lower left corner… enclosed in heavy and elegant, though plain envelopes… and sent by post in outside envelope." Ward (1878), p. 57, records the custom shifting: "P. P. C. cards are no longer left when the absence from home is only for a few months, as for the summer; nor are they left by persons starting in midsummer for a foreign country, **as residents are then supposed to be out of town**." That last clause is the whole seasonal migration in nine words, from a Philadelphia press, and it is quotable.
Ward also codes the etiquette of the summer colony (pp. 57–59): "At places of summer resort, those who own their cottages call first upon those who rent them; and those who rent, in turn, call upon each other, according to the priority of arrival."

**[B] The Philadelphia destination.** Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Atlantic City": https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/locations/atlantic-city/ — beach resorts including Cape May "steadily grew, catering to the affluent" through the first half of the nineteenth century; in 1854 Philadelphia developers laid the rail line to Absecon Island. Useful background, not evidence about this house.

**[A, negative-but-useful] The 1952 Social Register, Philadelphia.** https://archive.org/details/social-register-philadelphia_1952-12_unset — full text searchable. **There is no entry at 1822 Pine Street.** The same volume still lists socially registered households at 1801, 1805, 1809 and 1827 Pine (e.g. "Lowrie Miss Sarah D … 1827 Pine"). **[B] inference, clearly labelled:** by 1952 the block was still a Social Register street and 1822 was not a Social Register address — consistent with, but not proof of, its multi-unit status. Absence from the Register is never proof of anything on its own.

### 10. THE HOUSE NEXT DOOR CONVERTED FIRST — the sourced way to write "The Apartment House Years"

The repo's GAP 1 (1922–1941) has no documents. It does not have to be narrated from nothing. **1820 Pine Street — the adjoining house, and the 1914–15 Congressional Union headquarters — was advertising itself as apartments by 1919**, and those advertisements are free and dated.

- **[A]** *Evening Public Ledger*, Oct 9, 1919, p. 27: "**HOUSEKEEPING APARTMENTS / FINEST IN PHILADELPHIA / 1820 PINE STREET** … all the latest conveniences. One large [living] room with a beautifully designed open fireplace…" URL: https://panewsarchive.psu.edu/lccn/sn83045211/1919-10-09/ed-1/seq-27/

## ARTICLE IDEAS THIS LENS SUPPORTS

1. **"Thursday, 1822 Pine Street: how a Philadelphia at-home day worked."** Built on the Dec 1904 clipping the site already owns, Dreka's 1873 Philadelphia card-engraver's manual, and Ward's 1878 Philadelphia etiquette book. Salver in the hall, the servant screening at the door, the engraved reception day, the P.P.C. card that ended the season. Every claim sourced, none about people we can't name.
2. **"Wanted: a chambermaid, no wash, carfare paid."** The single 1917 want-ad, read closely — what "private family," "no wash," "references," "carfare paid" and "apply Monday a.m." each tell you about the working of this house — set against Eaton's 1897 numbers for Pine Street and the Thursday half-day. Honest about the ad's racial restriction.
3. **"Who actually lived here: the tenants the deeds don't show."** S. Decatur Smith at 1822 Pine in 1866, Guy Bryan Schott advertising from the address in 1870 — both A-grade, both free, both during the Roset ownership. A piece about the difference between owning a house and living in it, which is also a quiet correction of the site's own 1854–1893 framing.
4. **"A new story on the front and rear."** The 1899 campaign told properly for the first time: Duhring Okie & Ziegler at 1420 Chestnut, contract to J. S. Rodgers of Stanwick N.J., $17,500 paid for the house on April 3, plumbing rebuilt, and the unresolved storey-count puzzle laid out honestly for the reader.
5. **"The Philadelphia Exchange, Wednesday, May 10, 1893."** The auction behind the $14,000 headline — James A. Freeman & Co., a four-story residence, 22 by 90 feet — with a short explainer on how Philadelphia sold its houses at the Exchange.
6. **"Cellar to attic: the machinery of a Pine Street house in 1859."** Sloan's Design II as the period specification — furnace and registers, twelve-burner chandeliers in each half of the drawing room, copper-lined tubs, a dumbwaiter from the butler's pantry, a speaking tube to the kitchen, four inside bells, and porcelain knobs downstairs against mineral knobs in the attic. Explicitly framed as "the type, not this house."
7. **"The Waverly Street side."** Two faces of one lot, using the National Register's own sentence about principal streets versus the streets of "workingmen's housing, stables, and the rear buildings of the mansions," and the deed's "90 feet deep to Waverly."
8. **"The house next door went first."** 1820 Pine advertised as "Housekeeping Apartments — Finest in Philadelphia" in 1919 and at $125 a month in 1921. A defensible way to open the apartment-house chapter that invents nothing.
9. **"A sewing machine at 1822 Pine, 1866."** A short, charming piece on domestic technology, hung entirely on the Willcox & Gibbs testimonial list — with the 1866 Philadelphia gas price printed on the very same page.
